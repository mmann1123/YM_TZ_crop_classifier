# Deep learning in remote sensing applications: A meta-analysis and review

**Citation:** Ma, L., Liu, Y., Zhang, X., Ye, Y., Yin, G., & Johnson, B. A. (2019). "Deep learning in remote sensing applications: A meta-analysis and review." *ISPRS Journal of Photogrammetry and Remote Sensing*, 152, 166-177. DOI: `10.1016/j.isprsjprs.2019.04.015` (verified via Crossref; title, authors, journal match exactly).

> Review / meta-analysis paper — per skill rule 7, the per-paper "evaluation protocol" question is reframed: the relevant evidence is the *cross-study distribution* of reported accuracies by task and data type, and what it implies about where deep learning's advantage is largest vs smallest.

## Objectives

Quantitatively survey the state of deep learning (DL) in remote sensing by meta-analyzing 200+ publications (2014-2018). Two parts: (1) a statistical meta-analysis coding each study on 12 attributes (sensor type, study area, study target, DL model, processing unit, accuracy, training samples, spatial resolution, etc.); and (2) a narrative review of how DL is applied across image fusion, registration, scene classification, object detection, LULC classification, segmentation, and OBIA. Goal: identify where DL helps most, what limits it, and open challenges.

## Methods

- **Corpus:** 221 peer-reviewed journal articles (conference papers excluded for lower rigor); 176 of 402 retrieved were discarded as unrelated, leaving 165 for meta-analysis. Scopus search on "deep learning" AND "remote sensing."
- **Coding scheme (Table 1):** twelve attributes per study, including DL model (CNN/RNN/AE/DBN/GAN), processing unit (pixel/object), spatial resolution bucket (high <10 m, medium 10-30 m, low >30 m), study area type, training-sample count, and reported accuracy.
- **Analysis:** descriptive distributions (Figs. 1-6) of publication growth, study targets, DL-model usage, resolution, study area, and — the load-bearing figure — **box plots of overall accuracy for the three classification sub-targets** (LULC classification, object detection, scene recognition).
- **Evaluation-protocol framing (load-bearing for a review):** The paper aggregates *self-reported* overall accuracies across heterogeneous studies and protocols; it does **not** re-evaluate anyone under a common holdout, and it does not stratify by whether the underlying studies used spatially disjoint vs pooled splits. So the accuracy distributions reflect mixed, mostly in-scene/benchmark protocols. The key analytic move is comparing accuracy *across task types and data regimes*, not across leakage-controlled splits — useful for our argument precisely because it shows the regime dependence of DL's edge.

## Key Findings

- **DL's advantage is largest on curated benchmarks and smallest / most variable on medium-resolution LULC — our exact regime.** From the accuracy box plots (Fig. 6): **scene classification has the highest median OA (~95%)**, **object detection second (~92%)**, and **LULC classification the lowest and most variable (~91%)**. The authors attribute the high scene/object numbers to those subfields' "overwhelming use of benchmark datasets" specifically built for DL, whereas LULC studies map real, variable land-cover classes. This is the citable anchor: deep learning looks best where the data are curated benchmarks and least impressive, with the widest spread, on real medium-resolution land-cover mapping like Sentinel-2-scale crop/LULC work.
- **DL is data-hungry.** Supervised DL "usually requires a large number of training samples"; labeling for LULC is "highly time- or cost-intensive," motivating transfer learning, active learning, data augmentation, and unsupervised/GAN pretraining — all framed as workarounds for the label-scarcity that DL imposes. Even so, the median ~91% for LULC "is already significantly higher than... other classifiers (RF, SVM, decision trees)" *on the studies surveyed* — note this is an aggregate over mostly benchmark-leaning protocols, not a controlled head-to-head.
- **Resolution dependence:** DL is most used and most successful on high-resolution (<10 m / <2 m) imagery rich in spatial-structural features; the review notes the relative **scarcity of DL benchmark datasets for medium- and low-resolution imagery** and that applying conventional DL directly to medium-res (10-30 m, e.g. Landsat/Sentinel-2) is "difficult... because of the lack of such fine structures," prompting patch-based or DBN approaches.
- CNNs dominate (most common model), reflecting their fit to grid-arranged multiband imagery; RNN/DBN/GAN/RBM far less common. Most studies are urban; vegetation/water far fewer.
- Time-series analysis with DL is explicitly called out as rare and under-explored as of 2018 ("studies in this regard are very few"), with a couple of DNN-on-time-series crop examples cited — i.e. the engineered/temporal niche our paper occupies was sparsely populated in the DL literature at this point.

## Relevance to Our Crop-Classification Study

- **Quantitative support that DL's edge shrinks in our regime.** The "highest accuracy on curated benchmarks, lowest and most variable on medium-resolution LULC (~91%)" finding is directly usable to argue that benchmark-derived DL win rates over-state what DL delivers on real Sentinel-2-scale (10 m, medium-res) crop/LULC mapping — exactly where our classical engineered-feature pipeline competes. It tempers any "DL always wins" claim drawn from Indian-Pines-style results.
- **Data-hunger is a cost argument.** The review's repeated emphasis that DL needs large labeled sets (and the proliferation of augmentation/transfer/active-learning fixes to compensate) reinforces our smallholder-data-scarcity and cost-efficiency framing: in data-poor sub-Saharan settings, the regime that most favors DL (abundant curated labels) is absent.
- **Medium-resolution benchmark gap.** Their note that DL benchmark datasets for medium/low-resolution imagery are lacking helps justify why so many DL "successes" are on high-res or hyperspectral benchmarks rather than Sentinel-2 crop typing — and why our contribution (a crowdsourced, field-labeled, medium-res Tanzanian dataset + evaluation) is filling a real gap.
- **Temporal-features niche.** The 2019 observation that DL-on-time-series was rare positions engineered multi-temporal features (our `xr_fresh` statistics) as a still-underexploited, sensible representation rather than a relic — useful lineage/positioning.

## Evaluation Caveats

- **Aggregates self-reported accuracies across mixed protocols.** The accuracy box plots pool numbers from studies with heterogeneous and often in-scene/benchmark splits; the meta-analysis does **not** control for spatial leakage, field-grouped CV, or holdout disjointness. So the ~91% LULC / ~95% scene medians are protocol-uncontrolled and should be cited as evidence of *regime dependence*, not as leakage-clean accuracy figures.
- **No per-class / minority-class breakdown.** Overall accuracy is the meta-analyzed metric; the review does not aggregate macro-F1, per-class recall, or Kappa, so it cannot speak to minority-crop performance or imbalance artifacts (a silence worth noting — it inherits the OA-centric reporting of its source studies).
- **No spatial-transfer or cross-year stratification.** The meta-analysis does not separate studies by whether they tested spatial/temporal transfer, so it offers distributional context, not transfer evidence. It sits, as a body of evidence, at the in-scene/benchmark end of our spectrum.
- **2014-2018 snapshot.** Pre-dates transformers and most modern self-supervised RS foundation models; the "CNNs dominate, time-series DL rare" picture is dated, though the regime-dependence and data-hunger conclusions remain broadly relevant.
- **Comparative ML-vs-DL claim is indirect.** The statement that DL's ~91% LULC median exceeds RF/SVM is drawn from comparing across different studies, not a controlled benchmark — so it is weaker evidence than a head-to-head and should not be over-read as "DL beats ML on LULC."
