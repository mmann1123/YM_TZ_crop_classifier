# Smallholder maize area and yield mapping at national scales with Google Earth Engine

**Citation:** Jin, Z., Azzari, G., You, C., Di Tommaso, S., Aston, S., Burke, M., & Lobell, D. B. (2019). Smallholder maize area and yield mapping at national scales with Google Earth Engine. *Remote Sensing of Environment*, 228, 115–128. DOI: `10.1016/j.rse.2019.04.016` (verified against Crossref — title, authors, journal, and year all match).

## Objectives

Build "wall-to-wall" 10 m maps of (i) cropland presence, (ii) maize presence, and (iii) maize yield for the main 2017 season across the entirety of **Kenya and Tanzania** (~1.5 million km²), using Google Earth Engine to make the workflow scalable to national/continental extents. The explicit framing is that smallholder East-African systems are data-sparse, cloud-plagued, and heterogeneous, and the goal is a method that "will work in any smallholder region in any year, with as little dependence as possible on obtaining cloud-free conditions or having access to reliable ground measurements." This is the **same country, same crop, same smallholder/cloudy problem, and the same classical-ML-over-deep-learning premise** as our study — making it the single strongest direct comparator in the corpus.

## Methods

- **Sensors / features.** Both **Sentinel-1 (`VV`/`VH`, refined-Lee speckle-filtered, ratio/difference derivatives)** and **Sentinel-2** (13 bands plus a large bank of vegetation indices — `NDVI`, red-edge `RDNDVI`/`RDGCVI`, `GCVI`, `MTCI`, `REIP`, and the SWIR-based tillage/residue indices `NDTI`, `STI`, `NBR1`/`NBR2`, `CRC`). All processing in GEE.
- **Temporal representation.** For **classification**, three **seasonal median composites** ("water year" Oct→Sep, split into three groups), each band suffixed by season and stacked — yielding 24 features for `S1`, 69 for `S2`. This is a **composite/median temporal summary**, not raw per-date sequences fed to an RNN and not tsfresh-style per-pixel statistics, but it occupies the same "engineer phenological summaries, then feed a tree" philosophy as our `xr_fresh` features. For **yield**, a richer phenological representation: a **harmonic regression (discrete Fourier transform, first two harmonics)** fit per pixel to the `GCVI` time series, summarizing seasonal shape parametrically.
- **Feature pre-selection.** Mutual-Information ranking against labels, then iterative removal of any feature correlated > 0.8 with a higher-ranked feature — a lightweight, interpretable feature-selection step, the same spirit as our variance-threshold + SHAP-driven reduction.
- **Classifier.** **Random Forest** for both cropland (CRL) and maize (MZL) tasks (final GEE config `n_estimators=300`, `max_features='sqrt'`, `min_samples_leaf=10`). RF chosen explicitly for serializability/portability and resistance to overfitting. No deep learning anywhere in the pipeline — a deliberate classical-ML choice at national scale.
- **Labels / crowdsourcing precedent.** Crop-type labels (~maize/non-maize) drawn from One Acre Fund, TAMASA, AFSIS, IPA field data (2015–2017); crop/non-crop labels generated through an **in-house interactive GEE "labeler"** in which the authors hand-labeled thousands of random points (4,140 in Tanzania, 4,509 in Kenya) against high-resolution basemap imagery. This human-in-the-loop, platform-based labeling is a direct **precedent for our YouthMappers crowdsourced ground truth**. Area-based sampling (centroid for small fields, up to 5 points for large) limits over-representation of big fields — analogous to our field-size handling.
- **Yield model (SCYM).** A scalable crop-yield mapper that trains a regression on ~50,000 APSIM-Maize crop-model *simulations* (not on observed yields), grouped by Global Agro-Environmental Stratification (GAES) zones, using peak `GCVI` from the harmonic fit; weather (aWhere/CHIRPS/MODIS LST) and soil (HC27) covariates included.

- **Evaluation protocol (load-bearing).** This is the paper's standout — it uses **multiple, explicitly different splitting criteria, and is candid about each**:
  - The dataset is first held out as 80% (cross-validated) / 20% (final test) per country, per sensor-combination, per task.
  - For the **cropland (CRL)** task they ran *two* fold criteria: **(1) a random split, and (2) a district-based split "to ensure that test folds were geographically distinct from the training folds."** The district-based fold is a **spatially disjoint holdout — a higher bar than our field-wise grouped CV**, approaching true spatial transfer within-country.
  - For the **maize (MZL)** task: "given the clustered spatial distribution of the training data, we only used a **random split by field**" — i.e. **field-disjoint (FID-grouped) cross-validation**, which **meets our exact bar** (field-wise grouped CV within a region), and they report accuracy on the held-out 20% of points not used in training.
  - The **yield** model is validated against **fully independent ground-based crop cuts** (One Acre Fund, ~4,000 fields/year in W. Kenya) aggregated to **district level** — an out-of-domain, independently-measured holdout, the strongest validation of the three outcomes.
  - On the manuscript spectrum, this paper therefore spans **field-wise FID-grouped CV (maize) → spatially disjoint district holdout (cropland) → independent crop-cut yield holdout** — i.e. it **meets or exceeds our evaluation bar** on every reported outcome. It is one of the few corpus papers that does *not* commit the in-region-random-split-as-OOS conflation (for maize it explicitly grouped by field; for cropland it explicitly tested geographic separation).

## Key Findings

- **Cropland (CRL):** ~85% out-of-sample accuracy with `S1 + S2` in **both** Kenya and Tanzania; accuracy plateaus at ~20 features. Sentinel-1 was "particularly useful," reflecting the cloud problem.
- **Maize (MZL) — the candid, sobering numbers:** test accuracy **79% in Tanzania** and **63% in Kenya** (text reports 67% for Kenya at one point; abstract says 63%). Maize-from-other-crops is explicitly harder than crop-from-non-crop, and the MZL task "relied much more heavily on the Sentinel-2 features," with large accuracy gaps when trained on `S1` alone.
- **SWIR and red-edge dominate the Tanzania feature ranking — directly validating our band choices.** For Tanzania CRL, `SWIR1-S2`, `SWIR1-S3`, and `SWIR2-S1` ranked **first, second, and fifth**; the SWIR-based `STI`/`NDTI` indices ranked sixth and ninth. For the MZL task, SWIR-based indices (`NDTI`, `STI`, `SWIR-S3`) again placed in the Tanzania top features. This is strong independent evidence for our reliance on `B11`/`B12` (SWIR) and red-edge `B6`. Sentinel-1 ratio features mattered more in Tanzania than Kenya (attributed to fewer cloud-free optical images and stronger soil-wetness contrast in TZ).
- **Yield:** SCYM captured ~50% of yield variance at district level against crop cuts in W. Kenya; soil factors (esp. nitrogen) explained 72% of predicted-yield variation.
- **Country transfer is hard:** the selected feature sets for Kenya and Tanzania were almost entirely different (only Longitude common in the CRL top-10), underscoring that even within East Africa, cross-region models do not trivially transfer — a caution relevant to any claim about our single-region model's generality.

## Relevance to Our Crop-Classification Study

**The single strongest comparator in the corpus** — cite it as the benchmark our study is measured against:

1. **Same setting, same thesis.** Tanzania + Kenya smallholder maize, cloudy/heterogeneous, Sentinel-1/2 in GEE, **classical ML (Random Forest) chosen deliberately over deep learning** for scalability and portability. It establishes that the lite/classical approach is the field standard for exactly our problem, not a fallback.
2. **It sets the evaluation bar we cleared — and shows our headline is strong.** Because their maize accuracy is a **field-disjoint (random-split-by-field)** number, and their cropland accuracy includes a **district-disjoint spatial holdout**, their figures are *honest* in the way ours are. Their candid **maize accuracies (TZ 79%, Kenya 63%)** are the appropriate yardstick. Against that yardstick, **our Cohen's Kappa 0.82 / F1-micro 0.85 — over a much harder *multi-crop* problem (maize, cotton, rice, sorghum, millet, sunflower, cassava + non-crop), not a binary maize/non-maize split — looks strong**, and was achieved with optical-only inputs.
3. **Band/feature validation.** Their Tanzania feature rankings put **SWIR (`B11`/`B12`-derived `STI`/`NDTI`) and red-edge indices at the top** — independent corroboration of our `B11`, `B12`, and `B6` choices and of the value of SWIR-based residue/tillage information for East-African cropland.
4. **Crowdsourcing precedent.** Their in-house GEE point-labeler — humans labeling random points against high-res basemaps to build training data at national scale — is a direct methodological precedent for our **YouthMappers crowdsourced labeling**, and supports the claim that platform-based human labeling can produce national-scale, usable ground truth.
5. **Where they go further than us (be honest).** Their **cropland district-based split** and **independent crop-cut yield holdout** are *stronger* than our field-wise-grouped-CV-within-one-region protocol; we do not do a spatial holdout. Their cross-country feature divergence is also a caution that our single-region model may not transfer to a different agroecology — a limitation worth conceding and a candidate future direction.
6. **Feature philosophy.** They use seasonal-median composites (classification) and harmonic/DFT fits (yield) to summarize phenology before the tree — the same "engineer the temporal signal, then use a simple model" logic as our `xr_fresh` tsfresh-style statistics, just a coarser summary. Our richer per-pixel statistics (slope, skewness, peaks, complexity) are a natural extension of their compositing approach.

## Evaluation Caveats

- **Despite strong protocols, the maize number is binary, not multi-crop.** TZ 79% / Kenya 63% are **maize vs non-maize** accuracies; they do not resolve the hard minority crops (cotton, sorghum, millet, sunflower, cassava) that our study targets. Our multi-crop Kappa is therefore the harder achievement, and the comparison should foreground that the tasks differ in difficulty.
- **No per-class minority-crop metrics** for a full crop tail; the binary collapse sidesteps the class-imbalance problem rather than solving it. They report accuracy (and learning curves) but not macro-F1 or per-crop Kappa beyond the maize/non-maize binary.
- **Cross-year robustness only partially tested:** training mixed 2015–2017 labels onto 2017 imagery (assuming crop locations are stable year-to-year), and notes accuracy generally *dropped* when more cross-year data were added — a quiet warning about temporal transfer, not a clean cross-year evaluation.
- **Yield validation is district-aggregated** (forced by 10–30 m GPS error on field points), so field-level yield accuracy is not directly demonstrated; the honest holdout is at coarse spatial resolution.
- **Computational cost** is implicitly large (national scale, ~1 trillion pixels), handled by GEE; the paper does not frame this as a "lite" method the way ours does, though RF itself is lightweight. The portability/serializability argument for RF is the same efficiency argument we make.
- **SR vs TOA caveat for yields:** they could not obtain a standard Sentinel-2 surface-reflectance product at scale in GEE and used an empirical TOA→SR correction — a data-availability constraint specific to 2017/2018, less relevant now but a reminder that their pipeline predates routine L2A access.
