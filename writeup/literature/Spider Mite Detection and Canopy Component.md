# Spider Mite Detection and Canopy Component Mapping in Cotton Using Hyperspectral Imagery and Spectral Mixture Analysis

**Citation:** Fitzgerald, G. J., Maas, S. J., & Detar, W. R. (2004). Spider Mite Detection and Canopy Component Mapping in Cotton Using Hyperspectral Imagery and Spectral Mixture Analysis. *Precision Agriculture*, 5(3), 275–289. DOI: `10.1023/b:prag.0000032766.88787.5f` **(from Crossref title match — the DOI is NOT printed in the PDF; the PDF header carries only "Precision Agriculture, 5, 275–289, 2004". Crossref returns an exact title/author/venue/year match for this DOI, so it is treated as verified-by-title-match.)**

> **Scope note (rule 7):** This is a **crop-stress / pest-detection** study, NOT a crop-type-classification task. There is no train/test classifier, no class-imbalance accounting, and no spatial-transfer question in the crop-mapping sense — so the standard pooled-vs-field-wise-vs-spatial-holdout evaluation calibration does not apply. Per the authoring rules, the briefer focuses on **sensor design, feature design (spectral unmixing as an interpretable, low-parameter, no-DL feature method), and conceptual transfer** to our work, especially the illumination/geometry sensitivity that bears on our reflectance- and `hue`-based features.

## Objectives

Determine whether airborne hyperspectral imagery (AVIRIS), after **linear spectral mixture analysis (SMA / "unmixing")**, can discriminate a field of healthy cotton from an adjacent field of strawberry-spider-mite–damaged cotton, using a small library of reference endmember spectra collected in the field. A second goal is to produce spatially explicit **fractional-abundance maps** (healthy plant, soil, mite-damage, shade) showing the location and severity of stress for precision-agriculture (targeted pesticide) use.

## Methods

- **Sensors.** NASA **AVIRIS** (224 contiguous 10 nm bands, 400–2500 nm, 18 m ground pixel) flown over two 2.8 ha USDA cotton fields at Shafter, CA, on **four dates in 1999** at differing solar geometries. Ground-based reference spectra collected with a liquid-crystal tunable-filter (LCTF) camera at ~1 mm resolution to build "pure" endmembers.
- **Feature method — spectral mixture analysis (the conceptually load-bearing part).** Each pixel is modeled as a **linear combination of a small set of endmember reflectance spectra** weighted by fractional abundances that are constrained to sum to unity; the residual RMSE measures fit. Endmembers: healthy leaf (H), mite-damaged leaf (M), sunlit soil (S), shaded soil (Sh), plus optional dead leaf / cotton boll / within-canopy shade variants (9 total). A simplified **multiple-endmember SMA (MESMA)** lets the number/type of endmembers vary per pixel (2–4 per pixel), choosing the lowest-RMSE model. The authors emphasize SMA is "relatively simple conceptually, is not computationally intensive, and is readily available" — i.e. an **interpretable, low-parameter, physically grounded, no-deep-learning feature extractor**. The final consistently-best model used just S, M, H, Sh.
- **"Evaluation."** No machine-learning classifier and no train/test split. Validation is by (i) whether unmixing correctly separated the known mite-free vs mite-infested fields, (ii) agreement of abundance maps with weekly ground mite counts, and (iii) regression of fraction/index maps against an independent **yield monitor** map used as a surrogate for mite damage (yields 1338 vs 3310 kg/ha; mite counts significantly different; yield-vs-mite r² = 0.49). So "accuracy" here means physical plausibility and correspondence to ground truth, not classification accuracy.

## Key Findings

- **SMA successfully separated adjacent mite-damaged and healthy cotton fields**, and produced abundance maps that correctly located known damage, healthy canopy, and soil — demonstrating that a handful of interpretable endmembers can resolve a within-crop stress at field scale.
- **Illumination/geometry sensitivity is severe and is the central cautionary finding.** Solar zenith angle varied across the four flights (**22.7°, 27.9°, 49.3°, 38.9°**), and **shade fraction confounded the damage signal**: comparing two images only 4 days apart (28 Aug vs 1 Sep), raw mite-fraction values were 0.09 vs 0.01 (a 9:1 ratio that should have been near-identical); after **shade normalization** (`Ms = M/(1 − Sh)`) they became 0.20 vs 0.04 (5:1) — still distorted. Without forcing a shade endmember into every pixel, fractions failed to sum to unity (0.60–0.92). The paper repeatedly attributes day-to-day instability to **sun-angle / shading differences**, not to the crop.
- **The same spectral feature can change dramatically with viewing/illumination conditions.** Within-canopy soil/shade spectra showed a spurious **red-edge effect** from inter-leaf light scatter, forcing the authors to collect "pure" soil endmembers from outside the canopy. Reflectance is not a stable fingerprint of the surface alone; it carries a strong geometry term.
- **Soil was ruled out as a confounder** (NDVI-vs-yield and soil-fraction-vs-yield both r² ≈ 0 on the bare-soil date), strengthening the attribution of the signal to mite damage.

## Relevance to Our Crop-Classification Study

This paper is **conceptual, not a head-to-head comparator** (different sensor, different task, no classifier). Its value is twofold and both points strengthen our manuscript's framing:

1. **Spectral unmixing as a precursor to "lite," interpretable, no-DL feature engineering.** SMA is a low-parameter, physically interpretable, GPU-free method that turns high-dimensional spectra into a handful of meaningful fractional features — philosophically aligned with our argument that **engineered, interpretable features + simple models** (rather than opaque deep networks) can resolve subtle agronomic signals. It is an early, vivid example that you do not need representation learning to extract discriminative, explainable features from remote-sensing data; you need the right physically-motivated transform. This supports the inductive-bias half of our "lite learning" case and pairs naturally with our SHAP-based interpretability.
2. **A concrete warning about illumination/geometry sensitivity that directly bears on our `hue` and reflectance features.** Our pipeline uses HSV `hue` and raw reflectance bands (`B2`, `B6`, `B11`, `B12`) whose values, like the mite/shade fractions here, are sensitive to solar zenith, shadowing, and canopy geometry. This paper shows that a feature can swing by a factor of 5–9 between near-adjacent dates purely from sun-angle/shade — a strong reason that **our multi-temporal *statistics* (mean, min, slope, complexity over the season) are more robust than any single-date reflectance or hue value**, since aggregating across many dates averages out illumination-driven noise that would corrupt a single observation. It also cautions that `hue`, derived from a geometry-sensitive RGB transform, should be read as a relative seasonal descriptor, not an absolute one. The shade-normalization trick (`M/(1 − Sh)`) is an early analogue of why robust temporal summarization matters.

## Evaluation Caveats

- **Not a classification study** — no machine-learning model, no train/test split, no out-of-sample or spatial-holdout protocol, no class-imbalance question. None of the field-wise-vs-pooled-CV calibration applies; do not treat its "accuracy" as comparable to crop-type classification accuracy.
- **Tiny, controlled setting:** two adjacent 2.8 ha experimental fields, one cultivar ("Maxxa"), one pest, one site (Shafter, CA), 1999 — there is no generalization, transfer, or operational-scale claim, and the "validation" is essentially internal physical-consistency checking against ground counts and a yield-monitor surrogate.
- **The headline weakness is exactly the point we cite it for:** abundance fractions are **strongly confounded by solar geometry and shade**, demonstrably unstable across dates 4 days apart even after correction — a property that motivates multi-temporal feature aggregation rather than reliance on single-scene reflectance.
- **Hyperspectral / airborne** (224 narrow bands, AVIRIS) is far richer and far more expensive than our six broadband Sentinel-2 channels; the unmixing approach is not directly portable to our data, so the relevance is methodological/conceptual only.
- **No quantitative classification metric** (no Kappa, F1, OA) is reported or applicable; the strongest quantitative result is a yield-vs-mite-count r² of 0.49 and a soil-confounder r² ≈ 0.
